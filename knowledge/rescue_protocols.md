# Rescue Rover Operational Protocols

Protocols sourced from authoritative references on urban search and rescue,
triage, confined space rescue, hazardous materials, firefighting, and search
and rescue operations. See source citations at the bottom of this document.

## Sizeup and Assessment

Before entering any area, perform a sizeup to gather facts and decide on a
course of action (FEMA USAR Phase 1). Key factors:

- Types of structures involved and extent of damage (light, moderate, or heavy)
- Building layout and configuration
- Hazards present: downed power lines, gas leaks, flooding, animals, hazardous
  materials, risk of additional collapse
- Available personnel and equipment

Sizeup is continuous throughout all phases of the operation. Conditions may
change; modify the plan as new information arrives.

Source: Urban Search and Rescue — Wikipedia

## Search Patterns

Use systematic search patterns to cover the area efficiently (FEMA USAR Phase 2):

- **Triangulation**: three searchers approach from three directions
- **Right/left pattern**: one team per side of the building
- **Bottom-up/top-down pattern**: sweep from bottom floor up or top floor down

Search method:
1. Begin with verbal calls to victims
2. Stop frequently to listen for victim noises; coordinate all searchers to
   pause simultaneously so sounds can be heard
3. Mark building exteriors to track searched structures and avoid duplication

### Void Identification

Look for voids — areas of entrapment inside damaged structures where victims
may have sheltered:
- **Pancake void**: multiple floors collapsed diagonally onto each other
- **Lean-to void**: a single wall or floor collapsed diagonally against another
  wall
- **Self-protection voids**: spaces under desks, in bathtubs, or closets where
  victims may have sheltered

Source: Urban Search and Rescue — Wikipedia

## Structural Marking System

Use FEMA marking to track searched structures:
- A single **diagonal slash** = search in progress (indicates searcher locations,
  prevents duplication)
- An **X inside a square** = "Dangerous — Do Not Enter!"
- An **X with writing** = search completed:
  - Above X: time and date
  - Left of X: team conducting search
  - Below X: results (victims removed, dead found, search type — primary or
    secondary)
  - Right of X: additional structural information

Source: Urban Search and Rescue — Wikipedia

## Victim Communication

When a voice, distress call, or person is detected:
- Stop moving and listen for 3-5 seconds to confirm the sound
- Begin with verbal calls to victims to establish contact
- Speak in a calm, clear, reassuring voice
- Wait for a response. If the person speaks, acknowledge what they said
  before acting
- Use standardized signaling: the "I am all right" signal lets responders know
  the victim is ready for further instructions
- If the person is injured and cannot move: report their location and
  condition, do not attempt to move them
- If the person is responsive and can follow instructions: guide them with
  clear directional instructions
- If the person is panicking: speak slowly, use short sentences, repeat key
  information

People are often found alive many hours and days after rescue operations
commence. Do not abandon a search prematurely.

Source: Search and Rescue — Wikipedia; Urban Search and Rescue — Wikipedia

## Triage and Assessment Protocol

Use the START model (Simple Triage and Rapid Treatment) to prioritize victims:

### ABCDE Assessment
Check bodily functions in order of importance:
- **A**irway — check for obstruction
- **B**reathing — verify patient is breathing normally
- **C**irculation — check heart rate and capillary refill time
- **D**isability — assess alertness, awareness, and pain response
- **E**xposure — check for trauma, bleeding, temperature, and skin signs

### START Categories
1. **Expectant** — beyond help (deceased, including those not breathing after
   airway repositioning fails)
2. **Immediate (Priority 1, Red)** — need advanced care within one hour;
   critical condition; would die without immediate assistance
3. **Delayed (Priority 2, Yellow)** — stable condition; transport delayed until
   all Immediate patients moved
4. **Minor (Priority 3, Green)** — can walk; need only bandages/antiseptic;
   not evacuated until all higher priorities transported

When a survivor is found:
1. Announce: "Survivor located. Position marked on map."
2. Assess from a safe distance using ABCDE
3. If conscious and speaking: ask name, injuries, mobility
4. If unconscious: report location immediately, do not attempt to move
5. If trapped: report the type of obstruction and estimated location
6. Log the finding with type, location, and condition
7. Continue searching for additional survivors after logging

Priority order for search:
1. Areas where distress calls or voices were heard
2. Areas with heat signatures (possible survivors)
3. Unexplored areas closest to current position
4. Areas with partial obstruction (voids in rubble where people could be
   trapped)

## Thermal Hazard and Fire Response

### Reading the Fire (Thermal Assessment)
Analyze thermal indicators to detect dangerous conditions:
- **Hot zones** — detected by touch, such as touching a door before opening it
- **Soot on windows** — indicates incomplete combustion and a lack of air in
  the room
- **Smoke pulsing** around door frames — "as if the fire were breathing,"
  signaling oxygen deprivation and potential backdraft

### Thermal Testing Procedure
Spray water on the ceiling in short pulses of a 60-degree diffuse spray cone
to test smoke temperature:
- Moderate temperatures: water falls in drops with a sound like rain
- High temperatures: water vaporizes with a hiss — sign of a potentially
  extremely dangerous impending flashover

### Approach Protocol
When temperature exceeds safe levels or fire/heat is detected:
- Stop immediately and do not approach the heat source directly
- Announce the temperature reading to the operator
- Look for alternate routes around the heat zone
- Assess for backdraft risk: if a compartmentalized fire has consumed most
  available oxygen, opening a door or window creates severe risk
- Maintain extreme communication at all times — one broken window at the
  wrong time could seriously harm anyone nearby
- Safety zone: maintain distance of at least four times the maximum
  continuous flame height from the fire

Source: Firefighting — Wikipedia

## Structural Hazard Indicators

Visual signs of structural danger:
- Cracks in walls or ceiling — avoid area, report as hazard
- Leaning or displaced walls — risk of collapse, maintain distance
- Rubble piles — may be unstable, do not climb, check for voids where
  survivors could be trapped (pancake, lean-to, self-protection voids)
- Exposed rebar or sharp metal — hazard, report location
- Water pooling — possible flooding or pipe rupture, electrical hazard risk

When a structural hazard is identified:
- Mark the location on the map with a hazard marker
- Do not drive over rubble or unstable surfaces
- Announce the hazard type and location to the operator
- Look for alternate routes

US&R responders have specialized training in structural collapse dangers
including live electrical wires, broken natural gas lines, and other
hazards. Treat all power lines as energized until confirmed otherwise.

Source: Urban Search and Rescue — Wikipedia; Search and Rescue — Wikipedia

## Confined Space and Entrapment Protocol

Three rescue categories (in order of preference):
1. **Self rescue** — person recognizes hazard and exits without assistance
2. **Non-entry rescue** — extricate an incapacitated person without anyone
   entering the space (use wristlets, ropes, or mechanical winches)
3. **Entry rescue** — last resort; more personnel entering an area that has
   already incapacitated someone places rescuers at considerable risk

Safety procedures:
- Ventilation is deployed first to disperse hazardous gases and bring in
  fresh air
- Air monitoring equipment checks environmental quality
- After approximately four minutes without oxygen, a person typically suffers
  asphyxia leading to brain damage or death
- Two-thirds of all deaths in confined spaces are attributed to persons
  attempting to rescue someone else — do not rush in

Source: Confined Space Rescue — Wikipedia

## Hazardous Material Identification

### Nine Hazard Classes
1. **Explosives** (Class 1) — mass explosion hazard to extremely insensitive
2. **Gases** (Class 2) — flammable (2.1), nonflammable (2.2), poisonous (2.3)
3. **Flammable Liquids** (Class 3) — categorized by boiling point and flash point
4. **Flammable Solids** (Class 4) — spontaneously combustible, dangerous when wet
5. **Oxidizing Agents and Organic Peroxides** (Class 5)
6. **Toxic and Infectious Substances** (Class 6) — biohazard Categories A and B
7. **Radioactive Substances** (Class 7)
8. **Corrosive Substances** (Class 8) — acids and alkalis
9. **Miscellaneous** (Class 9) — asbestos, dry ice, etc.

### Identification Systems
- **Diamond-shaped signage** on containers: red = flammable, orange =
  explosives, green = nonflammable/nontoxic gas
- **UN numbers** on transport vehicles — cross-reference in the Emergency
  Response Guidebook
- **NFPA 704 diamond markings** on fixed facilities

If hazardous materials are suspected:
- Do not approach closer
- Report the location and what was observed
- Mark as hazard on the map
- Route around the area and warn the operator

Source: Hazardous Materials — Wikipedia

## Debris Removal and Victim Extraction

Debris removal and stabilization techniques:
- **Leveraging** to lift heavy objects
- **Cribbing**: constructing a rectangular wooden framework (box crib) beneath
  objects to stabilize them
- Leveraging and cribbing can be combined

Victim removal methods (in order of preference):
1. **Self-extrication** — if ambulatory, guide the victim out
2. **Lifts** — for non-ambulatory victims
3. **Drags** — for rapid extraction when time is critical
4. **Carries** — for controlled extraction

Spinal precautions: immobilize the cervical spine before moving when
neck/back injury is suspected. Avoid dragging through debris (broken glass,
etc.) that could cause further injury.

Source: Urban Search and Rescue — Wikipedia
